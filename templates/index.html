01 / INSTITUTIONAL CONTEXT

The Tara Ocean Foundation: Science for the Ocean

Created in 2003, the Tara Ocean Foundation is the first foundation in France officially recognized as serving the public interest dedicated exclusively to the ocean.

For over two decades, it has pioneered a dual mission: conducting high-level, open-access marine science in collaboration with premier international laboratories while raising public and political awareness about climate threats. By developing standardized sampling protocols from the surface down to the mesopelagic zone, Tara expeditions have built the largest genetic and environmental baseline of marine microorganisms ever assembled.

02 / WELCOME ABOARD TARA PACIFIC

Welcome Aboard the Tara Pacific Expedition

Between May 2016 and October 2018, the research schooner Tara set sail across the world's largest ocean to undertake one of the most extensive expeditions ever conducted on coral reefs and pelagic ecosystems.

Through this immersive digital platform, we invite you to relive this extraordinary journey. The expedition's primary objective was to study coral biodiversity, examine their capacity to adapt to global warming, and quantify human-induced pressures across 32 island systems and archipelagos.

Why was this expedition crucial? Although coral reefs cover less than 0.2% of the ocean floor, they support nearly 30% of all marine species and protect tropical coastlines. Understanding the coral holobiont (coral animals and their symbiotic microorganisms) under extreme thermal stress is vital for planetary survival.

03 / FLOATING LABORATORY

The Schooner & Interactive Sampling Architecture

I hope you don't get seasick. Let's take a quick tour of the boat together. At first glance, you might think Tara is a rugged 36-meter aluminum polar schooner built to withstand the harshest oceans. But beneath her sails lies a highly synchronized floating laboratory. During the Tara Pacific expedition (2016–2018), the vessel didn’t just cross the water; she continuously filtered it.

The 36-meter aluminum research schooner Tara under sail during the Pacific expedition.

Welcome aboard the Tara schooner!

The diagram on the right reveals what actually happens on deck. This work relies on two distinct rhythms:

(i) Continuous surface filtering: Even while sailing at full speed, surface water is continuously pumped through the hull directly into a cramped on-board laboratory. The ocean flows through the boat, allowing scientists to filter and preserve delicate genetic material 24/7 before it can degrade.
(ii) Sampling stations and Plunge: When the ship stops at a sampling station, it lowers heavy sensory equipment down to 1,000 meters deep, pulling the dark, unexplored layers of the water column right up to the deck.

Sea Surface Temp — SST (°C)

Controls metabolic rates and coral bleaching thresholds (>29°C). Reveals the contrast between temperate/upwelling waters and the Western Pacific Warm Pool.

Salinity — SSS (PSU)

Tracks the balance between evaporation and precipitation. Subtropical gyres are salty (~35.6 PSU) due to high evaporation, whereas Panama and equatorial zones are diluted by heavy tropical rains (~33 PSU).

Chlorophyll-a (mg/m³)

A direct proxy for phytoplankton biomass. Low values (<0.15) mark nutrient-poor "ocean deserts" (gyres), while spikes (>0.5) indicate coastal runoff or cold nutrient upwellings.

Trophic Regimes & Chlorophyll Zones

Oligotrophic Ocean Desert (< 0.15 mg/m³)

Ultra-clear, nutrient-depleted waters characteristic of the vast South and North Pacific subtropical gyres. Phytoplankton biomass is minimal at the surface, forcing the ecosystem to rely on a Deep Chlorophyll Maximum (DCM).

Mesotrophic Island Wake (0.15 – 0.5 mg/m³)

Moderate primary production. Often observed around Polynesian and Micronesian archipelagos where the island creates localized turbulence, bringing enough deep nutrients to the surface to sustain coral reef food webs.

Eutrophic / Upwelling Zone (> 0.5 mg/m³)

High primary production driven by cold, nutrient-rich water reaching the sunlit surface. These zones trigger massive diatom blooms, acting as major carbon sinks.

Tara Pacific expedition brief & synthesis of results

The Tara Pacific expedition (2016–2018) yielded unprecedented insights into the health and resilience of coral reef ecosystems. By coupling high-frequency underway sensors with deep CTD casts and genomic filtration, researchers discovered that coral symbiosis stability is tightly regulated by local hydrodynamic regimes and temperature anomalies.

Coral Adaptability: High genetic variability in isolated atolls revealed unexpected resistance to episodic marine heatwaves.
Planktonic Connectivity: Microscopic biodiversity gradients tracked across the South Pacific gyre demonstrated how island wakes stimulate localized primary production (the Island Mass Effect).
Plastisphere Pollution: Microplastic fragments were detected across 100% of sampled archipelagos, acting as vectors for microbial biofilms and invasive species.

MAIN SCIENTIFIC RESULTS & BIOPHYSICAL MECHANISM

The Limiting Nutrient (Nitrates vs. Chlorophyll-a)

1. The Limiting Nutrient (Nitrates vs. Chlorophyll)

A clear upward curve. Without nitrates, chlorophyll stays near zero (oligotrophic desert). As soon as nitrates increase through deep-water upwelling, chlorophyll explodes.

2. Gas Physics (SST vs. Dissolved Oxygen)

A near-perfect inverse linear correlation (downward line). Governed by Henry's Law: the warmer the water, the less dissolved gas it can hold—critical for climate change and ocean deoxygenation.

3. Thermal Stratification (SST vs. Chlorophyll)

The warmest waters (>28°C) have the lowest chlorophyll. Warm water is light and stays at the surface, blocking cold, nutrient-rich deep water from rising: the ocean is thermally stratified.

4. Climate & Coastal Footprint (Salinity vs. Chlorophyll)

High-salinity waters (>35.5 PSU) in evaporative subtropical gyres (Easter Island) are stable oligotrophic deserts. Lower salinity (33–34 PSU) near equatorial rains and river plumes (Panama, PNG) brings terrestrial nutrients that trigger phytoplankton blooms.

07 / SCIENTIFIC PERSPECTIVES

Going Further: From Tara Microbiome to Tara Polar Station

The Microbiome Mission & Future Arctic Drift

Following Tara Pacific, the Foundation launched the Mission Microbiomes (2020–2022) across the South Atlantic, Southern Ocean, and African coasts to investigate how the ocean's invisible microbial network responds to deoxygenation, nutrient runoff, and warming.

Looking ahead to 2026–2045, the Tara Polar Station—a drifting polar research vessel frozen directly into the high Arctic pack ice—will monitor polar biodiversity, sea-ice physics, and climate feedback loops at the epicenter of global warming.

Diatoms (Bacillariophyta) — The Silica Biological Pump

Morphology: Unicellular eukaryotic algae enclosed in a hydrated silicon dioxide shell called a frustule, perforated with nanopores for nutrient exchange.

Biogeochemical Role: Diatoms generate roughly one-fifth of the oxygen produced on Earth each year. Because their silica shells are dense, dead diatoms sink rapidly into the deep ocean, exporting organic carbon away from the atmosphere for centuries.

Tara Pacific Insight: Diatom abundance peaks in equatorial upwelling zones and coastal island wakes where deep silicate-rich waters reach the sunlit euphotic zone.

Copepods — Keystone Trophic Connectors

Ecological Role: Representing up to 70% of mesozooplankton biomass, copepods link microscopic primary producers (diatoms, dinoflagellates) to planktivorous reef fish, seabirds, and marine mammals.

Active Carbon Transport: Every night, billions of copepods ascend to the surface to feed and descend hundreds of meters at dawn to evade predators, actively respiring and packaging carbon into fast-sinking fecal pellets at depth.

Coccolithophores & The Mission Microbiome

Calcifying Phytoplankton: Coccolithophores (such as Emiliania huxleyi) surround themselves with microscopic plates of calcite (CaCO₃). They simultaneously drive the organic carbon pump (photosynthesis) and the carbonate counter-pump.

Sensitivity to Acidification: As anthropogenic CO₂ lowers seawater pH, carbonate ion availability drops, threatening coccolith formation and altering ocean albedo (cloud-forming DMS emissions).

Microplastics & Coral Reef Stress

Manta Trawl Findings: Surface tows (330 µm mesh) during Tara Pacific revealed widespread microplastic accumulation, particularly in convergence zones of the North and South Pacific subtropical gyres.

Biological Impact: Coral polyps mistake buoyant microplastic fragments for zooplankton prey. Furthermore, plastics harbor a distinct microbial biofilm (the Plastisphere) capable of transporting opportunistic pathogens across reef systems.
